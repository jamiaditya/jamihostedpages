Three plots, one court hall

Someone wants to rent our three joined plots and run a badminton and pickleball hall. This page explains the two ways we can do it, what can be built, and how to decide.

The decision in brief

Option A We rent out only the empty land. The tenant builds everything. We spend nothing and take no loan. Expected rent: ₹3–4 per sq ft of land, about ₹30–40k a month.

Option C We build the hall’s basic structure for about ₹50–55 lakh, mostly with a bank loan, and rent out land plus building. The tenant adds the courts and fittings. Rent: ₹15.5–16 per sq ft of floor, about ₹1.0–1.04 lakh a month. If we put the full rent towards the loan, it’s cleared in about 5 years.

The rule of thumb: Option C is worth it only if the tenant pays at least about ₹45,000 a month more for the building than he would for the empty land. If he won’t pay that much extra, Option A is better.

These are estimates for planning. Check them with a licensed building planner, a chartered accountant, your bank and a lawyer before signing.

What can be built

Building rules say we must leave some land open along each edge. These open strips are called setbacks. In Andhra Pradesh there’s no separate limit on how much of the plot a building can cover, so the setbacks decide the building’s size. A licensed building planner (an LTP) will confirm the exact widths. For now we assume 3 m (about 10 ft) on the three road sides and 2 m (about 6.5 ft) on the north side.

Optional upper level (mezzanine)

Not included in our ₹50–55 lakh building or in the rent. The hall’s roof is high enough to add it later over the toilets and reception, for about ₹6–9 lakh. If the tenant wants it, he can build it at his own cost, or we build it and charge about ₹7–8 per sq ft extra. Keep the staircase space in the ground-floor layout so the option stays open.

Why three courts and not four

Four courts would squeeze in only with very little space between them and no room left for toilets, reception or stairs. Three courts with proper spacing, plus the facilities, is the better use of the land.

What makes a good badminton hall

  • A high roof: at least 26 ft clear inside, ideally 30 ft, so high shots don’t hit the roof.
  • No skylights over the courts: sunlight in players’ eyes ruins the game.
  • No ceiling fans: moving air pushes the shuttle around. Use wall exhaust fans.
  • Dark walls behind the courts, and lights placed along the sides rather than straight above.
  • Pickleball too: a pickleball court is the same size, so the same courts can carry both sets of lines.

The two options

Option A: rent out only the land

We lease the empty plots. The tenant pays for and builds everything, and runs the hall.

  • Our money in: nothing. No loan.
  • Rent: ₹3–4 per sq ft of land, about ₹30–40k a month, rising 5% a year.
  • Lease length: 12–15 years, so the tenant can earn back what he spent on the building.
  • If he leaves: we get the land back and owe nothing to anyone.
  • At the end: the lease should say the building becomes ours free of cost, or he removes it and clears the land. We pick one.

Best when: we want zero risk, or the tenant won’t pay much extra for a ready building.

Option C: we build the shell, he fits it out

We build the basic structure (foundation, steel frame, roof, walls, toilets, power, water). The tenant adds the court flooring, lights, nets and furniture, and runs the hall.

  • Building cost: ₹50–55 lakh, for the 6,490 sq ft ground floor.
  • Our money in: about ₹10–11 lakh from the three families. A bank lends the rest, ₹40–44 lakh.
  • Loan: a normal 10-year loan (₹53–58k a month), paid off early with the full rent in about 5 years.
  • Rent: ₹16 per sq ft to start, ₹15.5 to close, about ₹1.0–1.04 lakh a month, rising 5% a year.
  • If he leaves: we still have to pay the loan until a new tenant comes. A large deposit protects us.
  • After the loan: once it’s cleared (about 5 years), the building is ours and the full rent comes to the families.

Best when: the tenant has money for the fittings but not the building, and will pay well for a ready hall.

How rent decides it

The key idea. In Option C we spend ₹55 lakh, take on a loan, and accept the risk of paying it if the tenant leaves. In return the tenant pays more rent than he would for empty land. The question is whether that extra rent is enough.

We call that extra the building premium: Option C rent minus Option A rent. Everything comes down to this one number.

Is ₹60k realistic for empty land?

Probably not. ₹60k is about ₹6 per sq ft a month, closer to what bigger cities ask. In Srikakulam, with the tenant also spending ₹80 lakh–1 crore to build, ₹2.5–4 per sq ft is more likely, which is ₹25–40k a month. Local brokers can confirm this.

What the tenant will accept

Look at it from the tenant’s side. If he built the ₹55 lakh structure himself, his own loan would cost him about ₹76,000 a month. So he should happily pay us up to about ₹75,000 extra for a ready building, because that’s cheaper for him than building it. That gives a clear range:

With land worth ₹30–40k in rent, the sweet spot for Option C is a building rent of about ₹85,000 to ₹1.1 lakh a month. Our asking price of ₹15.5–16 per sq ft (₹1.0–1.04 lakh) sits inside it.

One more limit: what the hall earns

Three courts plus coaching classes might bring the tenant ₹2.5–3.5 lakh a month. If rent takes more than about a third of that, the business gets shaky. A rent of ₹1.2 lakh would take 34–48%, which is too much. A shaky tenant is a bigger problem in Option C, because we still owe the bank.

Choose Option C when

  • The tenant agrees to a building premium of ₹45–75k.
  • He has money for the fittings but not the building.
  • A bank agrees to lend against our plots.
  • All three families can put in their share of ₹11 lakh and agree how to share the loan payments if the hall is empty.
  • He signs for at least 7 years and pays a deposit of 8–10 months’ rent.
  • We plan to keep the land for 15 years or more.

Choose Option A when

  • The tenant offers less than about ₹45k extra for the building.
  • His track record or finances are weak.
  • Banks won’t lend against empty plots.
  • Any family can’t commit money or share loan payments.
  • Permissions for a sports hall are still unclear.
  • We might want the land back within about 10 years.

Simplest way to find out: ask the tenant for two prices, one for the empty land and one for the ready building. The difference is the building premium. Enter both in the calculator below.

Does ₹15.5 hold up?

  • Building premium: ₹1,00,595 − ₹34,720 = ₹65,875 a month, inside the ₹45–75k window where both sides gain.
  • The tenant’s alternative: building it himself would cost him about ₹69k a month (₹50 lakh building) to ₹76k (₹55 lakh) in loan payments. Our premium sits near that limit, so expect bargaining. Hold the rate and offer rent-free months instead.
  • The hall’s income: ₹1 lakh is about 33% of an estimated ₹3 lakh a month. That’s on the edge, so ask for his booking and coaching projections.

    Tax, simply

    A common mistake: only the interest part of each loan payment reduces tax. The part that pays back the loan itself does not. Instead, the firm can reduce its taxable income by a share of the building’s cost each year (called depreciation, about 10% a year).
    • Option A: each family pays tax on its share of the rent at its own tax rate. No firm needed.
    • Option C: the three families form a firm (an LLP is safest). The firm owns the building, pays the loan and collects rent. It pays tax at about 31%, but only on what’s left after interest, depreciation and upkeep. In year one that’s about ₹50,000 of tax on ₹12 lakh of rent.
    • The firm’s profit reaches the families tax-free. The firm can also pay the families up to 12% interest on the money they put in, which can lower total tax.
    • One open question for the accountant: the tax department may treat the firm’s rent as “house property” income instead of business income. Then depreciation isn’t allowed and the year-one tax rises to about ₹1.3 lakh. The calculator has a setting for this.
    • GST: not needed while yearly rent stays under ₹20 lakh. Both options stay under it for now.
    • TDS: the tenant must cut 10% tax from rent above ₹50,000 a month and deposit it in our name. We get credit for it when we file returns.
    • Don’t hand the land over to the firm. Lease it to the firm instead. Giving it as capital can trigger stamp duty and capital gains tax, and makes it hard to take back later.

    Legal and approvals

    Check before agreeing anything

    • Is a sports hall allowed here? The layout is approved for houses. We must confirm with the local planning authority whether an indoor sports hall is permitted, or needs a change of land use.
    • Is the road wide enough? Non-residential buildings need a minimum road width, often 12 m. Our 40 ft road is about 12.2 m, just enough. Also check whether either road is planned to be widened, because that would eat into our land.
    • Clean papers: clear ownership, encumbrance certificates, updated name transfer and paid property tax for all three plots.

    Permissions to build

    • Treat the three plots as one site. Otherwise the open-strip rule applies between our own plots too and the hall won’t fit. The LTP will advise whether to formally combine the plots or apply jointly.
    • Fire department approval is needed. AP rules require it for halls on land over 500 m², or taller than 6 m. Ours is both.
    • A steel hall still needs regular building permission and a structural safety certificate.

    What the rental agreement must say

    • Registered, since any lease over 11 months must be.
    • A fixed monthly rent for “the premises as per the approved plan”, not a rate per sq ft, so there’s no argument later about how the area is measured.
    • Rent, yearly increase, deposit, and minimum period (lock-in) the tenant cannot leave before.
    • Who owns the building at the end, and what happens if the tenant leaves early.
    • The tenant can’t sublet without permission and can use the place only for sports.
    • Insurance for the building and for injuries to players.
    • Opening hours and noise limits, since neighbours will complain about early-morning and late-night play.
    • What counts as default, how long he gets to fix it, and how disputes are settled.
    • A personal guarantee from the tenant.

    Among the three families

    Put in writing: how rent is shared, who pays what towards the building and loan (Option C), how decisions are made, and what happens if one family wants to sell or step out. In Option C, the firm’s agreement covers this.

    Next steps

    1. Meet an LTP to confirm the open-strip widths, whether a sports hall is allowed, and how to treat the three plots as one site.
    2. Ask two or three local brokers what empty plots on 40–50 ft roads rent for.
    3. Ask the tenant for two prices: empty land, and ready building. Also ask his lease length, his past experience and proof of funds.
    4. Enter both prices in the calculator and agree among the three families on Option A or C.
    5. For Option C: ask two or three banks whether they’ll lend against the plots, and get fixed-price quotes from two or three steel building contractors.
    6. Accountant review of the tax treatment and the firm.
    7. Lawyer drafts the families’ agreement and the lease. Register the lease.
    8. Get building and fire approvals before any construction starts.

    Words used here

    Setback
    Land that must be left open along each edge of the plot. Nothing can be built on it.
    LTP
    Licensed technical person: an architect or engineer allowed to submit building plans to the authority.
    Shell
    The basic building without the sports fittings: foundation, frame, roof, walls, toilets, power and water.
    Fit-out
    Everything the tenant adds to make it a working hall: court flooring, lights, nets, furniture.
    Building premium
    How much more rent the tenant pays for land plus building than for land alone.
    EMI
    The fixed monthly loan payment. Part is interest, part pays back the loan.
    Lock-in
    The minimum period the tenant agrees to stay. If he leaves earlier, he still owes rent for that period.
    Deposit
    Money the tenant gives upfront, returned at the end. It covers us if he leaves suddenly or causes damage.
    Escalation
    The agreed yearly rent increase, here 5%.
    Depreciation
    A yearly allowance for the building wearing out. It reduces taxable income without any money leaving the bank.
    LLP
    Limited liability partnership: a firm where each partner’s risk is limited to what they put in.
    Mezzanine
    A partial upper floor inside a tall hall.

    Assumptions

    • Land at ₹15–16 lakh per cent. One cent is 435.6 sq ft.
    • Bank loans against property at about 9.5–11% a year (HDFC Bank and SBI, November 2026), with a processing fee of up to 1%.
    • Badminton courts in similar Andhra towns rent at ₹250–350 an hour. Three courts plus coaching might earn the tenant ₹2.5–3.5 lakh a month.
    • Steel badminton halls cost roughly ₹170–700 per sq ft depending on height and finish. The ₹50–55 lakh ground-floor building fits within that range.
    • Option A rent is taxed at an assumed 20% average rate for the families. The firm pays about 31.2%.
    • Open-strip widths are assumptions until an LTP confirms them.
